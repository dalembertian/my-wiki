# Cory Doctorow: The Reverse-Centaur Critique of AI

Cory Doctorow's argument, adapted from a lecture for his book *The Reverse Centaur's Guide to Life After AI*: the AI bubble will burst, most AI companies will fail, and the fight against AI harm should target the economic and legal structures driving it, not the technology itself.

## Centaurs vs. reverse-centaurs

A **centaur** is a person assisted by a machine (autocomplete, GPS). A **reverse-centaur** is a machine that uses a human as its "squishy meat appendage" — e.g. an Amazon driver surveilled and paced by AI cameras. Doctorow's thesis: AI tools are funded specifically to produce reverse-centaurs, not centaurs, because that's where the money is.

## Why AI is being hyped: the bubble mechanics

Monopolist tech firms need "growth stock" valuations (high price-to-earnings multiples) to keep paying key hires in stock and winning bidding wars. But once a company dominates its sector, it can't grow anymore — and the market punishes that brutally (he cites Facebook's $240bn single-day sell-off in 2022 over a growth miss). So dominant firms need a constant supply of new bubbles — crypto, metaverse, now AI — to keep the growth story alive. The AI bubble's *material basis* is this pattern, not necessarily a belief that AI will actually deliver.

## "AI can't do your job — but an AI salesman can convince your boss to fire you anyway"

Doctorow's central mechanism: AI doesn't need to be *good* to be profitable for its sellers — it needs to be *cheap enough that firing most of a skilled workforce and covering the mistakes is worth it*. His radiology example: instead of "AI catches a few more tumors, so keep all radiologists and go slightly slower," the pitch is "fire 9 of 10 radiologists, split the savings with us, and the survivor takes the blame when the AI misses something" — an **accountability sink**, where oversight responsibility is pinned on a human without giving them real ability to catch errors at superhuman speed.

He extends this to coding: AI hallucinates plausible-looking library names, and malicious actors register real packages under those exact hallucinated names as a supply-chain attack — a bug pattern indistinguishable from working code, which is exactly the kind of error a senior engineer (the highest-paid, first-fired-for-AI role) is best positioned to catch.

On AI art: it's not part of AI's business model (illustrator wage bills are negligible next to AI infrastructure costs) but serves as a hype engine — proof AI can "do creative work," aimed at making the broader public believe AI is more capable than it is. Doctorow's definition of art (an artist's intent transmitted into a medium) makes AI-generated images inherently "eerie" (borrowing Mark Fisher's term) rather than art in that sense, absent human-added intent.

## The copyright argument

Doctorow argues *against* extending copyright to restrict AI training (a common creator-advocate demand). Training involves scraping (legal), analysis/counting (not something requiring a license), and publishing statistical facts about works (also legal) — expanding copyright to block this would be a major, precedent-setting expansion of exclusive rights. And historically, expanded copyright hasn't redistributed money to creators — it's been captured by the handful of publishers/studios/platforms that dominate creative labor markets ("giving a bullied kid more lunch money").

His alternative: the US Copyright Office's existing rule that **AI-generated work cannot be copyrighted** (copyright is for humans only) already achieves the goal — any company that wants exclusive rights over AI output has to pay humans to do the creative work. Combined with restoring **sectoral bargaining** (illegal for most US workers since Taft-Hartley, 1947, but how the WGA won its AI protections), this creates real leverage without expanding IP law.

## What survives the bubble

Doctorow expects most AI companies and datacenters to fail, similar to WorldCom's fraud leaving behind usable fiber-optic infrastructure. Post-bubble AI residue: engineers skilled in applied statistics, cheap secondhand GPUs (useful to VFX artists, climate scientists), and open-source models doing narrow useful tasks (transcription, summarization, image editing) — the stuff that would have just been called "plugins" if it had arrived without hype. His closing image: AI as "asbestos in the walls" of tech society — a mess that will need excavating for a generation regardless of how the bubble resolves.

## Cross-reference

Doctorow's "AI can't do your job, an AI salesman can get you fired anyway" argument is a sharp counter-read of the same capability jump that [[AI Doomsday Scenario Rattles US Markets]] treats as real and [[Free Intelligence and Radical Abundance]] treats as liberating — Doctorow's claim is that the economic disruption is real but doesn't require the AI to actually be competent, just cheap and hard to hold accountable.

The "bubble mechanics" section above — dominant firms need a constant supply of new bubbles to sustain growth-stock valuations — is a *motive* argument that pairs with the *mechanism* pieces in [[The AI 'Debt Bomb' - Financial Engineering, Not Fraud]] (how the spending is actually financed) and [[The AI Boom in Charts (June 2026)]] (the resulting stock concentration and capex figures): Doctorow explains why the money keeps flowing regardless of whether AI delivers, those two explain where it's actually going and what it looks like from the outside.


Read against the grain of this page: [[The Origins of Generative AI]] assembles the capability history Doctorow is discounting, and [[Demis Hassabis and DeepMind's Path to AGI]] and [[The AI Revolution - From ANI to Superintelligence]] are the two most confident versions of the trajectory he rejects — the latter's entire ASI-risk framework is moot if he is right. [[The Choices We Make About AI Now Are Critical]] occupies the middle ground, taking the capability claims seriously while sharing his concern about who absorbs the harm. [[Mews's AI Agents for Hotel Front Desks]] is a useful test case: a real deployment where the humans stay in the loop by design, which is close to the arrangement Doctorow says vendors rarely actually deliver.


[[Is the AI Boom Real]] maps this argument's distinctive position — capability fake, harm real anyway — which is invisible on a single optimist-pessimist axis.

[[Team Structures for an Agentic World - Pyramid to Hourglass]] is a vendor-side answer to the radiology example. An AWS keynote names the move Doctorow predicts, cutting juniors and keeping a thin layer of "agent managers", as "the trap", and quotes AWS's own CEO calling it "the dumbest thing I've ever heard." It stops short of his position, though. Its target is still a smaller, senior-heavy team, and it defends the junior pipeline because the company will need future verifiers, not on labor grounds.

[[Who Will Check the AI]] uses the accountability sink as the labor-market version of its verifier problem. Doctorow's contribution there is to move the question from skill to blame: even a perfectly skilled reviewer fails when responsible for catching errors at machine speed.
