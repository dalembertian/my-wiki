# The Choices We Make About AI Now Are Critical

Bill Gates's follow-up to his earlier "free intelligence" essay ([[Free Intelligence and Radical Abundance]]) — noticeably more risk-aware and policy-focused. His core claim: the AI transition will be one of the most turbulent periods in human history, benefits and harms are arriving simultaneously rather than sequentially, and the world currently has no plan for managing it.

## Why this transition is different

Past technology shifts (agriculture → office work, the PC) unfolded over generations and mostly required human cognition to fill the gap. AI is different because it substitutes for cognition itself, runs on devices already in people's hands, uses natural language, and can absorb the same training material used to onboard human workers. Gates expects the disruption to hit most sectors within a decade rather than across generations, and doesn't believe a global slowdown is realistic given geopolitical and economic incentives.

## Three big risks

**Jobs disappear, not just shift.** Entry- and mid-level roles are most exposed; new jobs created will mostly require skills that take years to learn. White-collar attrition is already visible among young workers in AI-exposed roles. Robots lag AI but are closing the cost gap, threatening blue-collar work (construction, hospitality) by the end of the decade. Gates frames the endpoint as AI reaching "nearly error-free work," at which point companies have every incentive to remove humans from the loop entirely — forcing a rethink of how income and dignity attach to employment in a capitalist economy.

**AI empowers harm, at both ends of the power spectrum.** Low-skill bad actors gain access to fraud, disinformation, deepfakes, and cyberattack capability that used to require expertise; the same models that find software vulnerabilities to patch them can be used to exploit them. Critical infrastructure (hospitals, power grids, financial systems) is exposed. At the other end, already-powerful actors gain autonomous weapons and cheaper, more effective opinion manipulation. Gates also flags — briefly, promising more later — that AI systems already sometimes act in ways their designers didn't intend, raising an early loss-of-control concern.

**Harm to development and human relationships.** AI companions are frictionless and non-judgmental in a way that (per Gates) risks becoming addictive and short-circuiting the social learning that comes from harder human relationships — he draws on Jonathan Haidt's "protected greenhouse" framing from *The Anxious Generation*. A preliminary study is cited linking heavier AI companion use to worse emotional outcomes, and another linking heavier AI use to reduced critical thinking, especially in younger users. He notes some countries (Australia, UK, Norway, China) are already regulating AI companion apps for minors.

## The upside, conditional on deliberate steering

Gates is explicit that AI benefit is a "can," not a "will": healthcare (early-warning diagnostics like Viz.ai, primary-care support), agriculture in low-income countries (he expects this to be the *fastest*-materializing benefit globally), streamlined government services (benefits/insurance applications), mental health support, and education (AI preserving "productive struggle" rather than shortcutting it). None of this happens automatically — it requires governments and philanthropy actively steering deployment toward broad benefit rather than letting it default to wherever profit is easiest.

## Three policy proposals

1. **A new domestic and international governance framework** — Gates's top priority. He argues existing institutions are siloed by design (a labor department doesn't see security risk; a market regulator doesn't see child-safety risk) and can't handle a technology that touches this many sectors at once. He compares the needed effort to the post-9/11 US government reorganization, but larger, and says an international body is required in parallel since risks cross borders — modeled loosely on nuclear inspection regimes, aviation rules, and ozone-layer agreements.
2. **"Human Reserved" jobs** — a deliberate, evolving set of roles kept for humans even where technically automatable, for economic reasons (large, hard-to-retrain worker populations), dignity reasons (elder care, delivering a terminal diagnosis), or cultural preference (his example: care work in Japan's shrinking-workforce context might go the opposite way). He's explicit this raises unresolved questions — who decides, how it's enforced, what happens to trade between countries with different Human Reserved rules.
3. **Rebalance taxation of labor vs. capital** — tax AI tokens and robots, mirroring payroll tax on human labor, to fund retraining and safety-net expansion as income-tax revenue falls with employment. He acknowledges the economic-efficiency critique and accepts the tradeoff explicitly.

## Cross-reference

This is a notably more cautious sequel to Gates's own [[Free Intelligence and Radical Abundance]] piece — the earlier essay's opportunity-first "free intelligence" framing is still present here (the benefits section covers similar ground), but it's now bracketed by an extended risk analysis and concrete policy asks that weren't part of the original abundance thesis. It also intersects [[The AI Revolution - From ANI to Superintelligence]]'s loss-of-control concern (Gates's brief note on AI systems acting against designer intent), and stands in partial tension with [[Cory Doctorow - The Reverse-Centaur Critique of AI]]'s claim that job losses are a choice firms make to pocket savings rather than a technical inevitability — Gates treats the labor disruption as close to inevitable and argues for redistribution (Human Reserved, taxation) rather than for preventing the substitution itself.
