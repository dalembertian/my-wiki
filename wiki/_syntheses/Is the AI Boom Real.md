# Is the AI Boom Real?

Nine pages in this wiki argue about the same question and reach incompatible conclusions. This is a map of who claims what, where the disagreements are empirical, and where they are about values — because those need settling differently.

## The trap: one axis isn't enough

Sorting these pages on a single optimist↔pessimist line loses the most interesting position in the set. There are two independent questions:

1. **Is the capability real?** Will AI actually do the work being claimed for it?
2. **Does serious harm follow?** Displacement, financial contagion, concentration of power.

Most people assume these track together — capability real means disruption real, capability fake means panic overblown. [[Cory Doctorow - The Reverse-Centaur Critique of AI]] occupies the cell that assumption erases: **capability fake, harm real anyway.** His mechanism is that AI doesn't have to work, it only has to be cheap enough that firing most of a skilled workforce and eating the errors pencils out — with the surviving employee positioned as the "accountability sink" who takes the blame. If he is right, waiting for capability to be disproven is not protective, because the harm never depended on capability.

## Where they actually agree

More than the rhetoric suggests, and the agreements are the load-bearing parts:

- **The money is real and enormous, whatever it buys.** Nobody disputes the capex. The argument is over what's being bought.
- **Something breaks if returns don't arrive.** Doctorow says the bubble bursts and the companies fail; the Debt Bomb page agrees the risk is real — it disputes the *shape*, arguing this is precedented off-balance-sheet financing rather than fraud, and that a datacenter, unlike a failed drug trial, leaves behind land, power, and compute. Bezos's "industrial bubble" framing: the kind that leaves useful infrastructure behind.
- **Displacement doesn't require AGI.** Doctorow and Gates arrive here from opposite premises — Doctorow because firing decisions don't wait for competence, Gates because entry-level cognitive work is exposed well before anything approaching general intelligence.

## Empirical vs. values

Separating these matters, because they don't resolve the same way:

**Empirical, and checkable on a timeline:**
- Is there a compute glut? The Debt Bomb page says no — 36% capacity growth against a record-low 1.4% vacancy, with only ~17.8% of working-age adults using generative AI. That's a falsifiable claim with a clock on it.
- Does agentic deployment actually work at scale? [[Why Most Enterprise Agentic Projects Are Doomed]] says mostly not, so far.
- Are the capability curves still bending? [[The AI Boom in Charts (June 2026)]] is the baseline to re-measure against.

**Values, and not settled by any amount of data:**
- Who should absorb the transition cost. Gates proposes taxing AI and robots and reserving categories of human work; Doctorow argues the fight belongs at the level of labor and competition law, not the technology.
- Whether a bubble that leaves behind infrastructure is an acceptable way to fund a build-out — the railway/fiber precedent cuts both ways depending on whether you were the investor or the beneficiary.

## The ground-truth checks

The wiki's most useful contribution to this argument isn't another opinion — it's the pages that report what actually happened, and they undercut *both* extremes:

- **Against "it's all hype":** [[When ChatGPT Broke an Entire Field - An Oral History]] documents a research field genuinely upended, with 19 researchers describing career-existential disruption. [[The Last Solo Programmers]] reports the same pressure in software.
- **Against "displacement is imminent":** [[Why Most Enterprise Agentic Projects Are Doomed]] finds most deployments failing for organizational reasons, and [[Mews's AI Agents for Hotel Front Desks]] is a working deployment that keeps humans in the loop by design — closer to Doctorow's "centaur" than the reverse-centaur he says the money demands.
- **The capability-vs-verification split** developed in [[The Harness Is the Product]] is the sharpest available resolution of the apparent contradiction: where a mechanical ground truth exists, agents deliver at remarkable scale; where it doesn't, they mostly don't. That predicts uneven, domain-by-domain displacement rather than either a uniform wave or a uniform fizzle.

## What would change the picture

Concretely, watch: datacenter vacancy rates reversing; whether enterprise agentic success rates move; whether the labs' harness revenue materializes as recurring or one-off; and whether displacement shows up in domains *without* mechanical verification, which is the case none of the current pages predicts well. The history these arguments extrapolate from is in [[The Origins of Generative AI]] — worth remembering that its own lesson is that consensus was wrong for a decade at a time, in both directions.
